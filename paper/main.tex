\begin{abstract}
Evolution strategies (ES) are often described as cheap to distribute because
workers exchange scalar fitnesses rather than gradients. That description
omits where the population-weighted update is contracted. We compare two
placements: gather fitnesses and repeat the contraction on every device, or
contract local population shards and all-reduce a model-sized partial update.
Using one implementation of seed-regenerated full-rank and never-materialized
low-rank perturbations, we measure the crossover on an 8$\times$A100 node and
a TPU v5e-8. The model-sized all-reduce wins every measured dense and
seed-regenerated cell; repeating the contraction returns as the better choice
when low-rank perturbations make that work small. The crossover moves with the
accelerator and recurs, with both signs intact, on Qwen2.5-0.5B. The low-rank
rewrite also pays more, not less, on the TPU: matched rank-1 cells cost
$0.05$--$0.18\times$ the materializing dense baseline on the v5e, versus
$0.13$--$0.42\times$ on the A100. The synchronization shared by both
placements is one fitness gather; centered-rank shaping then performs a
replicated local sort.

We separately test whether the faster perturbations preserve useful update
directions. Synthetic measurements give an approximately square-root law for
cosine alignment with the true gradient. On 0.5B- and 1.5B-parameter models,
the same law predicts full-rank alignment with the true teacher-forced-NLL
gradient within 10\%, while a low-rank correction calibrated at 0.5B predicts
1.5B alignment within 26\%. On a decoded-reward Countdown task, a three-seed
experiment resolves no quality difference between full rank and ranks 1, 4,
and 16 at equal training samples; rank 1 uses 33\% less steady-state time per
update. Scripts, configurations, result records, and environments are archived
with the released instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The update direction.} ES constructs a direction of improvement by weighting
random perturbations by how well they scored \citep{wierstra2014,nesterov2017}: with parameters $\theta$, noise
$\epsilon_i$, scale $\sigma$ and fitness $f$, the update is proportional to
$\frac{1}{N\sigma}\sum_i w_i\,\epsilon_i$, where the $w_i$ depend on the
fitnesses $f(\theta + \sigma\epsilon_i)$. With raw fitnesses, the standard
Gaussian estimator targets the gradient of a smoothed objective and carries a
dimension-dependent variance cost \citep{nesterov2017,duchi2015,berahas2022}.
This paper instead uses centered ranks \citep{salimans2017}: fitnesses are
replaced by normalized ranks, making the update invariant to strictly increasing
transformations of fitness but changing its estimand. We therefore call cosine
with an automatic-differentiation gradient \emph{update--gradient alignment},
not estimator bias or accuracy. No gradients flow through the ES model; the
gradient exists only as an offline reference in the validation experiments.

For comparing populations, define the sampling dimension $d_{\mathrm{samp}}$
as the number of independent scalar random variables in one perturbation. It
equals the full parameter count for dense and seed-regenerated noise. For a
rank-$r$ perturbation of an $m\times n$ matrix it contributes $r(m+n)$, while
non-matrix leaves contribute their full size. Section~\ref{sec:endtoend}
measures alignment against $N/d_{\mathrm{samp}}$; cross-rank comparisons are
also reported at equal $N$, because $d_{\mathrm{samp}}$ is a property of the
sampler rather than the dimension of the model gradient. It counts
independent draws; it does not measure how a rank-$r$ perturbation's support
overlaps a full-rank gradient, and Section~\ref{sec:endtoend} meets that
difference as an empirical correction factor rather than deriving the
variable that would absorb it.

\paragraph{The two recent algorithms.}
\citet{qiu2025} keep the perturbation exact and make its \emph{storage} free:
each candidate's noise is regenerated from a random seed whenever it is
needed (once to evaluate, once to assemble the update), so nothing
model-sized is ever kept per candidate, at the price of generating every
perturbation twice. Their headline demonstration is full-parameter
fine-tuning of billion-parameter language models with populations as small
as 30. EGGROLL \citep{eggroll2025} instead makes the perturbation cheap: each
weight matrix's noise is the product of two thin factors, so per-candidate
storage and compute shrink by the rank ratio, at the price of the noise no
longer being full-rank; it reports roughly $100\times$ throughput gains and
populations in the tens of thousands. Both samplers draw exactly from
their own distributions; the prices are paid in different currencies
(regeneration compute against the perturbation's full-dimensional
support), which is why neither paper subsumes
the other and why measuring them in one system is informative at all.

\paragraph{Why flattening forecloses structure.} A rank-$r$
perturbation is a statement about one weight \emph{matrix} (its noise is a
product of an $m\times r$ and an $r\times n$ factor). A library whose core
object is the flattened population, a dense (population, parameter-count)
matrix of materialized noise, has nowhere efficient to attach that
statement: the flattening is invertible (\texttt{ravel\_pytree} returns an
unflattening function), but a lazy, never-materialized per-matrix
perturbation is incompatible with an API whose contract is that the dense
noise matrix exists. This is a design commitment, not a bug. The instrument
therefore bypasses the flattened population representation rather than
patching it.

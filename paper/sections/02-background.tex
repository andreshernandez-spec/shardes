\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The estimator.} ES estimates a direction of improvement by weighting
random perturbations by how well they scored \citep{wierstra2014,nesterov2017}: with weights $\theta$, noise
$\epsilon_i$, scale $\sigma$ and fitness $f$, the update is proportional to
$\frac{1}{N\sigma}\sum_i w_i\,\epsilon_i$ where the $w_i$ are a function of the
fitnesses $f(\theta + \sigma\epsilon_i)$ (in this paper, centered ranks \citep{salimans2017}: sort
the fitnesses and replace each by its normalized position, which makes the
update indifferent to the scale of the loss). No gradients flow through the
model; the model only ever runs forward. The estimator targets the gradient
of a Gaussian-smoothed objective, and its variance carries a
dimension-dependent cost \citep{nesterov2017,duchi2015,berahas2022}. Rather
than assume an asymptotic formula for that cost, Section~\ref{sec:endtoend}
measures directional accuracy against the population-to-effective-dimension
ratio $N/d_{\mathrm{eff}}$.

\paragraph{The two 2025 algorithms.}
\citet{qiu2025} keep the perturbation exact and make its \emph{storage} free:
each candidate's noise is regenerated from a random seed whenever it is
needed (once to evaluate, once to assemble the update), so nothing
model-sized is ever kept per candidate, at the price of generating every
perturbation twice. Their headline demonstration is full-parameter
fine-tuning of billion-parameter language models with populations as small
as 30. EGGROLL \citep{eggroll2025} instead makes the perturbation cheap: each
weight matrix's noise is the product of two thin factors, so per-candidate
storage and compute shrink by the rank ratio, at the price of the noise no
longer being full-rank; it reports roughly $100\times$ throughput gains and
populations in the tens of thousands. Both samplers draw exactly from
their own distributions; the prices are paid in different currencies
(regeneration compute against the perturbation's full-dimensional
support), which is why neither paper subsumes
the other and why measuring them in one system is informative at all.

\paragraph{Why flattening forecloses structure.} A rank-$r$
perturbation is a statement about one weight \emph{matrix} (its noise is a
product of an $m\times r$ and an $r\times n$ factor). A library whose core
object is the flattened population, a dense (population, parameter-count)
matrix of materialized noise, has nowhere efficient to attach that
statement: the flattening is invertible (\texttt{ravel\_pytree} returns an
unflattening function), but a lazy, never-materialized per-matrix
perturbation is incompatible with an API whose contract is that the dense
noise matrix exists. This is a design commitment, not a bug, and it is why
expressing both 2025 algorithms efficiently required a new core rather
than a patch.

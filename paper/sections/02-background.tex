\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The estimator.} ES estimates a direction of improvement by weighting
random perturbations by how well they scored: with weights $\theta$, noise
$\epsilon_i$, scale $\sigma$ and fitness $f$, the update is proportional to
$\frac{1}{N\sigma}\sum_i w_i\,\epsilon_i$ where the $w_i$ are a function of the
fitnesses $f(\theta + \sigma\epsilon_i)$ (in this paper, centered ranks: sort
the fitnesses and replace each by its normalized position, which makes the
update indifferent to the scale of the loss). No gradients flow through the
model; the model only ever runs forward.

\paragraph{The two 2025 algorithms.}
Qiu et al.\ keep the perturbation exact and make its \emph{storage} free:
each candidate's noise is regenerated from a random seed whenever it is
needed (once to evaluate, once to assemble the update), so nothing
model-sized is ever kept per candidate, at the price of generating every
perturbation twice. EGGROLL makes the perturbation itself cheap: each weight
matrix's noise is the product of two thin factors, so per-candidate storage
and compute both shrink by the rank ratio, at the price of the noise no
longer being full-rank. The two prices are paid in different currencies
(compute against exactness), which is why neither paper subsumes the other
and why measuring them in one system is informative at all.

\paragraph{Why flattening forecloses structure.} A library that first
concatenates all weights into one vector can only ever perturb that vector
elementwise. A rank-$r$ perturbation is a statement about one weight
\emph{matrix} (its noise is a product of an $m\times r$ and an $r\times n$
factor); once the matrix has been flattened into a segment of a long vector,
there is no object left to attach that statement to. This is a design
commitment, not a bug, and it is why expressing both 2025 algorithms required
a new core rather than a patch.

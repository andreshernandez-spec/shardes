\section{Reference implementation boundaries}
\label{sec:refimpl}
Two implementation-level differences from the reference code, moved here
because they are boundary conditions rather than results. The reference's
rank-1 path does not compile on a 16\,GB TPU chip at $d{=}512$,
$N{=}16384$: the compiler allocates 24.1\,GiB of temporaries,
reproducible with no TPU via the compile-only probe in the repository,
the same pathology this instrument diagnosed and works around. The workaround
pads rank-1 factors with one zero column on TPU so the compiler retains a fused
matrix multiply; its numerical footprint and regression check are archived, and
the same cell runs here at 245\,ms. The pad is TPU-only: the GPU compiler keeps
these dots monotonic in rank, and padding there only costs time. The reference also declines embedding tables
outright, while the instrument carries that seam
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). Both are properties of the released
implementations, not of the algorithms.

\section{Stability exploration}
\label{sec:stability}
This experiment is a stress test, not a characterization: one task, one
150-step horizon, one RL recipe, three seeds per point, and only the
endpoints of each perturbation interval. It lives in the appendix
because its question (comparative optimizer robustness) is separate from
the paper's systems argument; its metrics and falsification criteria
were nevertheless fixed in a committed protocol before the data.

The relevant stabilization mechanisms have distinct lineages. Target networks
address value-learning divergence
\citep{baird1995,tsitsiklis1997,mnih2015,vanhasselt2018,zhang2021target}; the
recipe tested here instead carries a clipped policy objective and a
reference-model KL penalty
\citep{kakade2002,schulman2015trpo,schulman2017ppo,ouyang2022}. Empirical work
questions which ingredients are load-bearing
\citep{engstrom2020,andrychowicz2020,orz2025}. Reports of ES-induced forgetting
also motivate reporting drift beside apparent robustness
\citep{abdi2026forgetting,schweighofer2026awd}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{f8-robustness.png}
  \caption{Stability under hyperparameter perturbation. Each
    algorithm's own published settings are multiplied by the x-axis factor;
    y is held-out reward at a 150-step horizon, three seeds per point, red
    crosses mark collapse below the base model. GRPO at $8\times$ its
    published learning rate collapses to zero reward on every seed; ES
    shows no collapse at either tested endpoint of the same 64-fold
    learning-rate interval, and its one failure mode (noise scale
    $4\times$) is a freeze at the base model, not a loss of it. The
    purple markers are single-stabilizer ablations that trained fine,
    reported with the same prominence as the collapses.}
  \label{fig:f8}
\end{figure*}

ES carries neither the clipped objective nor the KL anchor in the tested
recipe, so we asked whether their absence is load-bearing. The answer has two
halves (Figure~\ref{fig:f8}).

First, the half we expected: multiplying the learning rate by 8 destroys
GRPO on every
seed (reward 0.000, below even format compliance, policy entropy zero)
while the same multiplier leaves every ES seed at or above its
published-settings reference; neither algorithm collapses at $1/8\times$,
so the finding is about the aggressive endpoint of the tested 64-fold
interval, whose interior was not explored, and it is not a stability
margin. The comparison is per-family by construction: each knob is
multiplied relative to its own published setting, and equal multipliers
do not correspond to comparable parameter-update magnitudes across ES
and GRPO. Descriptively, and not as a
matched rate (the twelve perturbed runs per algorithm perturb different
knobs per family): zero ES collapses against three GRPO collapses, and
the one ES failure (noise scale $4\times$) is a freeze at the base-model
floor.

Second, the half our own falsification criteria caught: removing GRPO's KL
anchor entirely, or widening its clip until it never binds, changed little
at this horizon on this rule-based verifier. At this one setting the
single stabilizers are not individually load-bearing; interactions
between stabilizers, and other settings, were not tested. The only collapse
observed was at the aggressive learning-rate endpoint, but the experiment does
not identify learning rate as the unique source of fragility. It is consistent with
prior reports that clipping is not
what makes PPO work \citep{engstrom2020,andrychowicz2020} and that
critic-based PPO trains without a KL penalty \citep{orz2025}.

Stability is also not retention. The drift row of the same experiment shows the
$8\times$-learning-rate ES runs ending roughly $50$ units of parameter
$\ell_2$ from the pretrained weights, against $0.3$--$2$ for healthy GRPO
runs. The experiment separates update robustness from parameter
retention; it does not establish that drift causes the robustness. The same
large-drift regime is where ES-induced forgetting has been reported
\citep{abdi2026forgetting}, though later work finds that degradation to
be largely recoverable drift rather than permanent forgetting, and not
specific to ES \citep{schweighofer2026awd}; the stress test's numbers
should be read beside both.

\section{Reproduction}
Every figure and generated table regenerates from a committed script against
a committed results directory, and every reported result traces to a script,
configuration, and record. Each results directory's README documents the
session that produced it (platform, commit, environment, cost, and any
incident). The figure and table map: F1, F2 and F2b from
\texttt{plot\_paper.py}; F4 from \texttt{plot\_cost.py}; F5 from
\texttt{experiments/phase0/plot.py}; F7 and F7b from \texttt{plot\_e13.py}; F8 from
\texttt{plot\_e14.py}; F9 from \texttt{plot\_e17.py}; the appendix grids
(M1--M3, both platforms) from
\texttt{plot.py} against each platform's results directory;
Tables~\ref{tab:tb1} and~\ref{tab:tb1b} from \texttt{tb1.py};
Table~\ref{tab:tb8} from \texttt{tb8.py}; Table~\ref{tab:tb7} from
\texttt{multihost/tb7\_e18.py}; Tables~\ref{tab:tb3}
and~\ref{tab:tb3b} from \texttt{tb3.py};
Table~\ref{tab:tb4} from
\texttt{analysis\_e15.py}; Table~\ref{tab:tb5} from
\texttt{tb5\_e17.py}; and Table~\ref{tab:tb6} from
\texttt{tb6\_e16.py}. Each table
script accepts \texttt{--latex} and writes the exact file this document
inputs. The per-collective byte counts quoted in
Section~\ref{sec:contraction} come from \texttt{comms.py}, which reads the
compiled HLO, and \texttt{tb2.py} prints them for every cell; they are two
numbers per placement across the whole grid, so the paper states them rather
than tabling them. Table~\ref{tab:tb6}'s predicted columns are the committed E1 fit
and the 0.53 correction, both frozen (rounded) in \texttt{e16-gate.yaml}
and \texttt{e16-stage2.yaml} before the runs; nothing in them is fit to
E16's measurements. The test
suite runs on CPU with no network; the multi-device logic is exercised on
eight simulated devices, so correctness properties (including the
device-count invariance test) are checkable without any accelerator, and the
two TPU compile probes
(\texttt{probe\_lr1.py}, \texttt{probe\_eggroll\_tpu.py}) reproduce
the compiler-level claims from any Linux machine. Source and result records
are at \url{https://github.com/andreshernandez-spec/shardes}. With the project
extras installed, \texttt{make -C paper} regenerates all generated tables and
builds the PDF; the provenance paragraph in Section~\ref{sec:setup} names the
frozen submission tag once assigned.

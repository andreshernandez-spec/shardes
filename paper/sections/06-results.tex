\subsection{Scaling to eight devices}
The full strong- and weak-scaling grids for both platforms are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:grids} (Figures~\ref{fig:m1-a100}--\ref{fig:m2-tpu}),
with weak scaling as parallel efficiency per (strategy, contraction)
pair, each series against its own smallest measured device count. This
paragraph states what they establish before the placement result. No
cell at $d{=}2048$ scales superlinearly. The grids do
hold efficiencies above one, all on the v5e at $d{=}512$:
mirrored rank 1 at $N{=}1024$ reaches $1.22$
at $D{=}2$ and $1.09$ at $D{=}8$, and that cell is also the $8.9\times$
between Tables~\ref{tab:tb1} and~\ref{tab:tb1b}. There the single-chip
point is the slow one: per-member throughput at $N{=}1024$ sits below
that at $N{=}256$ on one 16\,GB chip, so splitting the population relieves
a memory-bound baseline rather than beating a well-used one. The A100
has no such cell, and Table~\ref{tab:tb1b}'s $3.2\times$ for the same arm
at $d{=}512$ on the A100 is strong scaling of a small cell under the
reference-comparison protocol, not the weak-scaling efficiency quoted
next, which is at $d{=}2048$. At $d{=}2048$ and 32 members per device,
the low-rank arms hold
$0.86$--$0.93$ efficiency at eight devices on the A100 and
$0.71$--$0.91$ on the v5e. What degrades is a placement, not a
perturbation strategy: under contraction A the dense and seed-regenerated
arms fall to $0.39$ and $0.50$ on the A100 ($0.18$ and $0.54$ on the
v5e), because A replicates the full contraction on every device and those
two strategies have the most contraction work to replicate; the same
strategies under B hold $0.79$--$1.00$. The dense baseline's B placement
has no one-device point at $d{=}2048$, $N{=}256$ on the v5e, a memory
ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:f4} maps it). As one concrete reading of that
strong-scaling cell, the
mirrored rank-1/A arm falls from 102 to 19.9\,ms per generation on the A100
and from 67.8 to 9.73\,ms on the v5e between one and eight devices: $5.1\times$
and $7.0\times$ speedups, respectively. The instrument scales as a
sharded population should; what follows is the placement result.

\subsection{Which contraction placement wins?}
Figure~\ref{fig:f2} tests the directional prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:contraction}. B wins outright wherever every
candidate carries real contraction work (the dense and seed-regenerated
strategies), and wins harder on the TPU; for the low-rank family the two
placements are much closer (B/A between 0.92 and 1.15 on the A100, and
between 0.92 and 1.46 on the v5e) and the sign flips inside the grid, with A
ahead at $d{=}2048$ by 4--15\% on the A100 and 17--46\% on the v5e. Both
halves match the prediction: B's
advantage grows with per-candidate work, and A returns where that work
becomes small; its trajectory in device count is monotone
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2b}). The folk claim resolves as true but incomplete:
A moves only scalars, but B all-reduces a model-sized buffer and still
wins whenever the contraction it avoids costs more than those bytes.
Strategy A's scalar payload is exactly twice the naive count
(Table~\ref{tab:tb2}: two $4N$-byte gathers, the raw fitnesses for
shaping and the shaped weights for the contraction).

\paragraph{Bytes into time.} What decides a cell is not the payload but what
the payload costs, and the two differ by a rate that varies with message size
and with the fabric. Table~\ref{tab:tb7} times both collectives in isolation
on both platforms, as the marginal cost of one more collective inside a
program that is already running rather than as a program of its own: the
one-op floor is 0.32\,ms on the A100 and 0.60\,ms on the v5e, twelve and a
hundred times the in-program latency, so it is the marginal number a
generation pays. B's model-sized all-reduce costs 0.93\,ms on the A100 and
2.01\,ms on the v5e at $d{=}2048$, and 0.12\,ms on both at $d{=}512$; the
fitness gather both placements share stays under 10\,$\mu$s everywhere the
grid runs, and is under 21\,$\mu$s even at $N{=}2^{18}$.

Against the grid the two sides separate cleanly. In every cell B wins, its
all-reduce is 0.7 to 3.6\% of the advantage it buys, so the model-sized
transfer is not what decides the dense side; the contraction it avoids is.
In every cell A wins, the same all-reduce is 57 to 155\% of B's deficit, so
the low-rank side of the crossover is the collective and almost nothing else.
That also accounts for the platform shift, which the byte count cannot: the
same 96\,MiB payload costs $2.17\times$ more on the v5e, and A's low-rank
lead grows from 4--15\% there to 17--46\%. The fabric moved, the bytes did
not.

Three of those cells go past 100\%, and that is the model's open term rather
than a rounding artifact. B is slower in them than its local contraction plus
the isolated all-reduce can account for, by 0.6 to 0.7\,ms. A collective
issued inside a generation that is holding a model's worth of live buffers is
not the collective the ladder times in an otherwise empty program, and the
difference is measured here but not derived
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Every number in this paragraph is also one
host with a fat fabric: an NVLink node and a v5e host are where a 100\,MB
all-reduce is cheapest, and the commodity clusters the scalar folk claim was
made for \citep{salimans2017} are not measured here.

\subsection{Where the low-rank rewrite pays}
EGGROLL's motivation is explicitly GPU-shaped \citep{eggroll2025}, so the natural guess was that
a TPU, whose matrix unit makes dense multiplies comparatively cheap, would
narrow the gap or invert it. Figure~\ref{fig:f4} answers: the rewrite pays
\emph{more} on the v5e, not less, both in time (rank-1 at
$0.05$--$0.18\times$ dense against $0.13$--$0.42\times$ on the A100) and
in feasibility (the 16\,GB chip renders the dense baseline infeasible over
most of the grid, drawn as wins because feasibility is the strongest form
of paying off). The platforms move the constants, never the ordering, at
the matched cells of the measured grid. The
comparison is shape-for-shape: no v5e workload was reduced to
accommodate its smaller memory. The same figure prices Qiu's bet
\citep{qiu2025} honestly: seed regeneration costs
$5$--$6\times$ the materializing baseline per device on the TPU against
$1.3$--$3\times$ on the GPU, and its family (mirrored or not) is the
only one that never runs out of memory on either platform, which is
precisely the storage-for-compute trade it advertises. Whether that
$5$--$6\times$ is random-number throughput or the loss of the shared base
multiply the materializing arm keeps is not diagnosed here; the
measurement is end-to-end.

\subsection{Reference implementation comparison}

Matched shapes, matched populations, token-position evaluations per
second; nothing here claims
step-equivalence between implementations. The arms differ in antithetic
pairing, shaping and normalization details by design, and the honest unit of
comparison is throughput at the same shapes, which is how both papers report \citep{qiu2025,eggroll2025}.

Beyond parity, the measurements locate two differences from the reference
implementations, stated with their caveats. First, none of the references
shards: at eight devices the instrument reaches
$6\times$ the reference's single-chip ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:tb1b}), which
is a statement about released code, not about what the papers' internal
systems could do. Second, choices the references hardcode are measured to
matter: the contraction placement flips winner by platform
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2}), and EGGROLL's antithetic pairing is built into its
sampler, while the unpaired variant it cannot express has about $1.34\times$
greater update--gradient alignment at matched population in the large-$N$
regime its own paper targets (the fitted alignment curves behind
Figure~\ref{fig:f5}, and $1.36\times$ on the real model,
Table~\ref{tab:tb4}); the pairing halves factor storage in exchange, and at
small $N$ the difference is inside the replicate spread, so this is a choice to expose, not
a defect. Two further implementation-level differences (a TPU compile
failure in the reference's rank-1 path, and its declining embedding
tables) are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:refimpl}; they are
properties of the released implementations, not evidence that the
underlying algorithms require the same restrictions.

Three rows carry the table's weight. Single-device, the instrument runs
within 3\% of EGGROLL's own implementation on the A100
($0.97$--$1.02\times$ across the shapes) and at $0.68$--$0.85\times$ on
the v5e; that is an observed implementation gap between a general API and
a specialized kernel, platform-shaped rather than uniform, and the
experiment does not isolate its cause. At eight
devices it reaches $6\times$ their single-chip ceiling, because no
reference shards and the rows say so rather than hiding it. And evosax, the
incumbent, runs out of memory at $d{=}2048$ on a 16\,GB chip at every
device count: the flattening design decision, measured as a failure row
rather than argued from source code.

\subsection{The shaping barrier, priced}
Section~\ref{sec:design} declared one deliberate synchronization point;
here is its bill, measured in isolation across 84 cells on the TPU. The
communication half is not the story anywhere in the measured range: the
gather runs at
2.4--6\,$\mu$s, flat in $N$ (the payload at $N{=}2^{18}$ is 1\,MB) and
about 3\,$\mu$s dearer at $D{=}8$ than at $D{=}1$. The cost that grows is
the sort, and it is local compute paid redundantly rather than
communication: 12.1\,ms at $N{=}2^{18}$, identical to within $0.3\%$
between one device and eight, because every chip sorts the same
replicated array, about 46\,ns per member on this chip. At populations
from the tens \citet{qiu2025} run to a few thousand, the
whole barrier is under 60\,$\mu$s against generation times in the
milliseconds; at the tens of thousands EGGROLL targets, the sort's
linear growth puts it near a millisecond. It becomes a leading term
only where $N$ reaches the
rank-$r$ regime's extremes and the per-member work is small; at
$N{=}2^{18}$ a 12\,ms sort would rival a low-rank generation itself, and
distributed shaping would become a separate design problem.

An isolation number is the barrier's worst case, since a real generation
may overlap part of the latency behind compute, so we also closed the
caveat in context: identical full generations with shaping on and off,
at the largest cells the sweep measured on this platform, differ by
41\,$\mu$s ($d{=}512$, $N{=}1024$) and 149\,$\mu$s ($d{=}2048$,
$N{=}256$) in the median, both inside their own repeat spreads. At
measured cells the experiment does not resolve the barrier above
generation-to-generation repeat variation (seven repeats per point); the
12\,ms sort stands as the ceiling for a population
regime where a full generation does not fit this hardware anyway.

\subsection{Precision and datatype}
The complete systems-ablation table is in
Appendix~\ref{sec:systems-ablations}.
Two rows earn prose. Compute dtype is a memory choice more than a speed
one in these cells: bfloat16 against float32 is within a few percent on
the TPU, and within 12\% on the A100 (bfloat16 ahead by 8--12\% for the
dense and seed-regenerated arms, parity for low-rank), because at default
matmul precision the tensor core and the matrix unit already downgrade
the multiplies. Matmul
precision, by contrast, is strategy-shaped: forcing full-precision
multiplies is nearly free for the strategies whose time is the shared
base-model multiply, and costs $1.6$--$1.9\times$ for the low-rank
family, whose per-candidate correction multiplies are the operations being
upgraded. A practitioner running defaults gets Figure~\ref{fig:f4}'s
speedups; our scaling sweeps, which pin full precision for cross-device
comparability, were conservative against the low-rank arms specifically.

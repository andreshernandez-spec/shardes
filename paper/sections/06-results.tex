\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Scaling to eight devices}
The full strong- and weak-scaling grids for both platforms are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:grids} (Figures~\ref{fig:m1-a100}--\ref{fig:m2-tpu}),
with weak scaling as parallel efficiency per (strategy, contraction)
pair, each series against its own smallest measured device count. This
paragraph states what they establish before the placement result. No
cell at $d{=}2048$ scales superlinearly. The grids do
hold efficiencies above one, all on the v5e at $d{=}512$:
mirrored rank 1 at $N{=}1024$ reaches $1.22$
at $D{=}2$ and $1.09$ at $D{=}8$, and that cell is also the $8.9\times$
between Tables~\ref{tab:tb1} and~\ref{tab:tb1b}. There the single-chip
point is the slow one: per-member throughput at $N{=}1024$ sits below
that at $N{=}256$ on one 16\,GB chip, so splitting the population relieves
a memory-bound baseline rather than beating a well-used one. The A100
has no such cell, and Table~\ref{tab:tb1b}'s $3.2\times$ for the same arm
at $d{=}512$ on the A100 is strong scaling of a small cell under the
reference-comparison protocol, not the weak-scaling efficiency quoted
next, which is at $d{=}2048$. At $d{=}2048$ and 32 members per device,
the low-rank arms hold
$0.86$--$0.93$ efficiency at eight devices on the A100 and
$0.71$--$0.91$ on the v5e. What degrades is a placement, not a
perturbation strategy: under contraction A the dense and seed-regenerated
arms fall to $0.39$ and $0.50$ on the A100 ($0.18$ and $0.54$ on the
v5e), because A replicates the full contraction on every device and those
two strategies have the most contraction work to replicate; the same
strategies under B hold $0.79$--$1.00$. The dense baseline's B placement
has no one-device point at $d{=}2048$, $N{=}256$ on the v5e, a memory
ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:f4} maps it). As one concrete reading of that
strong-scaling cell, the
mirrored rank-1/A arm falls from 102 to 19.9\,ms per generation on the A100
and from 67.8 to 9.73\,ms on the v5e between one and eight devices: $5.1\times$
and $7.0\times$ speedups, respectively. The instrument scales as a
sharded population should; what follows is the placement result.

\subsection{Which contraction placement wins?}
Figure~\ref{fig:f2} tests the directional prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:contraction}. B wins outright wherever every
candidate carries real contraction work (the dense and seed-regenerated
strategies), and wins harder on the TPU; for the low-rank family the two
placements are much closer (B/A between 0.92 and 1.15 on the A100, and
between 0.92 and 1.46 on the v5e) and the sign flips inside the grid, with A
ahead at $d{=}2048$ by 4--15\% on the A100 and 17--46\% on the v5e. Both
halves match the prediction: B's
advantage grows with per-candidate work, and A returns where that work
becomes small; its trajectory in device count is monotone
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2b}). The grid is $3\times2$ per platform with holes at the
memory ceiling, which resolves regions and the sign changes between them
rather than a contour: read a crossover here as the cell where the sign
turns, not as a value of $N$ or $d$. The folk claim resolves as true but
incomplete:
A moves only scalars, but B all-reduces a model-sized buffer and still
wins whenever the contraction it avoids costs more than those bytes.
Strategy A's scalar payload is exactly twice the naive count, two $4N$-byte
gathers rather than one (Section~\ref{sec:contraction}).

\paragraph{Bytes into time.} What decides a cell is not the payload but what
the payload costs, and the two differ by a rate that varies with message size
and with the fabric. Table~\ref{tab:tb8} times both collectives in isolation
on both platforms, as the marginal cost of one more collective inside a
program that is already running rather than as a program of its own: the
one-op floor is 0.32\,ms on the A100 and 0.60\,ms on the v5e, twelve and a
hundred times the in-program latency, so it is the marginal number a
generation pays. B's model-sized all-reduce costs 0.93\,ms on the A100 and
2.01\,ms on the v5e at $d{=}2048$, and 0.12\,ms on both at $d{=}512$; the
fitness gather both placements share stays under 10\,$\mu$s everywhere the
grid runs, and is under 21\,$\mu$s even at $N{=}2^{18}$.

Against the grid the two sides separate cleanly. In every cell B wins, its
all-reduce is 0.7 to 3.6\% of the advantage it buys, so the model-sized
transfer is not what decides the dense side; the contraction it avoids is.
In every cell A wins, the same all-reduce is 57 to 155\% of B's deficit, so
the low-rank side of the crossover is the collective and almost nothing else.
That also accounts for the platform shift, which the byte count cannot: the
same 96\,MiB payload costs $2.17\times$ more on the v5e, and A's low-rank
lead grows from 4--15\% there to 17--46\%. The fabric moved, the bytes did
not.

The cells that fall short of 100\% are the model's open term rather than a
rounding artifact, and there are eight of the eleven. In each, B is slower
than its local contraction plus the isolated all-reduce can account for,
which a contraction cannot be. Timing the contraction apart from the
generation that carries it, at the same cells and the same discipline, says
why, and the answer is both of the available reasons at once.

The contraction does not shard at $1/D$. Measured as
$C/(D\,C_{\mathrm{local}})$ it reaches $0.60$--$1.01$ on the dense and
seed-regenerated arms and only $0.18$--$0.46$ on the low-rank family. What
separates them is the size of the contraction rather than the strategy: at
$C$ of 3.6--61\,ms the per-device fixed cost amortises and B keeps nearly
all of its ideal saving, while at $C$ of 0.15--1.3\,ms it does not. And the
same collective costs more in a leaner program: the 96\,MiB psum runs at
$1.75$--$1.80\times$ the ladder's isolated step inside the low-rank programs
against $1.15$--$1.41\times$ inside the dense ones, the low-rank generation
having less compute to hide the transfer behind. Both errors push the same
way and both are largest where the contraction is smallest, which is the side
of the grid A wins.

Replacing the two modelled terms with the measured ones closes those cells.
The four low-rank $d{=}2048$ cells fall from residuals of $0.98$--$1.17$\,ms
to $0.012$--$0.224$\,ms, and across the grid the median absolute residual
falls from 1.13 to 0.63\,ms with sign agreement at 18 of 20. Neither
mechanism suffices alone. What remains unexplained sits on the other side.
Bootstrapping the noise on each cell's difference of medians, which is the
quantity the residual is a residual of, nine of the twenty residuals exceed
twice it, so the model is not merely absorbing timing jitter. That noise is
measured within one process after one compilation, so it is a floor on the
true run-to-run uncertainty and nine is correspondingly an upper bound on how
many cells are genuinely unexplained. Two stand clear of the rest by an order
of magnitude either way, both \texttt{iid\_gaussian} at $d{=}512$, at
$10\times$ and $34\times$ their noise, where A is 1.5 and 5.4\,ms slower than
the contraction accounts for. Rerunning both on a second node of the same type
keeps one and dissolves the other: the $N{=}256$ residual reproduces at
1.6\,ms, while the $N{=}1024$ residual becomes $-0.6$\,ms once that cell's own
contraction is remeasured, so it was a property of the host rather than the
placement. The natural explanation for what survives, that A's replicated
contraction competes with the still-resident materialized population, is
refuted rather than merely unsupported: holding a population-sized buffer
during the isolated measurement, up to 3\,GiB per device, moves it by under
$0.31\%$ at every cell. The other candidate, that the isolation harness
feeds the contraction from a carry where a generation feeds it from the
evaluation, fails too: on a single device, where placement A's contraction is
the same computation it is at $D{=}8$, making the weights depend on the
evaluation is about 1\,ms faster than handing them in, on a machine where a
mechanism of this size would have shown as roughly 7\,ms. One cell is therefore
unexplained with both of its candidate explanations measured and rejected, and
the rest of the grid is small residuals on a side where the sign was never in
doubt. The same decomposition on the v5e finds
both mechanisms again, at the same sizes: $C/(D\,C_{\mathrm{local}})$ of
$0.95$--$1.00$ on the dense and seed arms against $0.16$--$0.62$ on the
low-rank family, an in-situ all-reduce at $1.18$--$1.60\times$ the ladder,
and sign agreement at 15 of 16 cells. That the two fabrics differ by
$2.17\times$ on the isolated 96\,MiB all-reduce and still fail in the same
two ways is the reason to read them as properties of the placement rather
than of one machine. Every number in this paragraph is also one host with a
fat fabric: an NVLink node and a v5e host are where a 100\,MB all-reduce is
cheapest, the dense-side result is therefore B's easy case, and the commodity
clusters of hundreds of workers the scalar folk claim was made for
\citep{salimans2017} are where it may well hold. The boundary is where the
fabric term grows by one to two orders of magnitude, so it is the one change
that could move the crossover rather than shift it, and
Section~\ref{subsec:boundary} crosses it.

\paragraph{Placement outcomes on the real model.} The same question,
re-measured outside the simplified block. Figure~\ref{fig:f9} times one
full ES update per cell of Qwen2.5-0.5B on a TPU v5e, on the frozen
prompt batch of Section~\ref{sec:endtoend}: ask, evaluate teacher-forced NLL,
and tell once, over four perturbation arms and four populations, with three
directional predictions frozen before the run.
All three hold. Both signs transfer from the block and widen in device
count: the seed-regenerated arm's B advantage grows monotonically to
$\log_{10}(t_B/t_A) = -0.164$ at $D{=}8$, and the low-rank arms flip to A
at all 18 shapes that fit, reaching $+0.225$, stronger than the block showed
at its largest model size. The third prediction was the falsifiable one,
that A's advantage should shrink as the rank rises, because the
per-candidate contraction work B avoids grows with $r$ while its all-reduce
does not: rank 16 is below rank 1 at every population they share
($+0.177$ against $+0.225$ at $N{=}32$, $+0.004$ against $+0.066$ at
$N{=}128$), approaching a tie from A's side without crossing. The wider
grid also shows what two populations could not, that the low-rank advantage
shrinks with $N$ as well, and for the same reason: raising the population
feeds A's per-candidate cost while leaving B's model-sized transfer
unchanged. Figure~\ref{fig:f9} gives the ratio between the
placements; Table~\ref{tab:tb5} in the appendix gives the level, the faster
placement's wall clock at every cell of the same grid, so the two together
determine both placements everywhere. The levels are worth reading beside the
ratios: the seed arm's update falls from 4.9\,s to 673\,ms across the eight
devices at $N{=}32$ and still costs 4.6\,s at $N{=}240$, while the low-rank
arms run at 71--272\,ms wherever they fit at all.

\paragraph{What the memory column measures.} The hollow markers double as a
finding, though not the one they look like. Rank 1 does not fit one 16\,GB
chip even at $N{=}32$ while the seed arm runs at every cell, which invites
the reading that carrying factors is what costs memory. Extending the grid
to three ranks refutes it: all three exceed HBM at identical shapes, with
XLA's reported temporaries agreeing to 0.1\%, and stored perturbations
would scale with $r$. The feasibility boundary is instead a pure function of
members per device, and the term is per-member activations. What sets how
many are live is the evaluation, not the perturbation: the seed arm scans
its population one member at a time, while the low-rank arms evaluate the
whole per-device population in a single batch, which is exactly where their
speed advantage comes from, since the base weight is unbatched under the
$\mathrm{vmap}$ and members share one multiply. Giving the seed arm the same
batching reproduces the low-rank memory curve. So the trade is real but runs
opposite to the obvious reading: the arm with the cheap perturbation is the
one that runs out of memory, because it buys speed by batching members, and
batching members costs activations. This leaves the ratios above untouched,
since A and B are the same arm at the same shape, but it does mean a
cross-arm wall-clock comparison prices two evaluation strategies as well as
two perturbation schemes.

\paragraph{Scope of the real-model anchor.} This anchor is one accelerator: the real-model
crossover was measured on the v5e only, so it confirms that the block's signs
survive a real model and not that they survive on both platforms. Every
cross-accelerator statement in this section rests on the controlled block
instead, where the 16\,GB and 80\,GB ceilings sit at different cells and
matched shapes are what make the two comparable.


\subsection{Across a real host boundary}
\label{subsec:boundary}
The crossovers above are one NVLink node, and the sharpest untested variable
is the host boundary, where a model-sized all-reduce leaves NVLink for the
cluster fabric.
We measured it on two $8\times$A100-SXM4-80GB nodes of a rented Instant Cluster,
same block workload, both placement predictions frozen before the run
(\texttt{experiments/phase2/multihost}). The cluster's InfiniBand did not carry
NCCL traffic on any host we drew, so the inter-node transport is TCP sockets
over the overlay; the preflight ladder measures it at $9.2$\,GiB/s against
NVLink's $515$\,GiB/s, a boundary at the slow end of the inter-node range.
Table~\ref{tab:tb7} gives $\log_{10}(t_B/t_A)$ at three topologies: one node
(\texttt{1x8}, $D{=}8$), the same eight devices split across the boundary
(\texttt{2x4}), and sixteen across it (\texttt{2x8}, $D{=}16$).

Three things follow. First, the boundary is a cliff for the placement that
crosses it: at fixed $D{=}8$, turning it on (\texttt{1x8} to \texttt{2x4}) moves
the seed-regenerated $d{=}2048$ cell from $-0.23$ to $+0.43$, about $108$\,ms
added to strategy B's generation as its all-reduce drops from NVLink to
$9.2$\,GiB/s. Second, that cliff flips the winner: the seed-regenerated arm at
$d{=}2048$ wins as B on one node and loses to A across the boundary at both
populations, while its $d{=}512$ cell keeps B everywhere (its all-reduce is
cheap) and rank~1 stays A with the margin widening to $+1.44$. The decision rule
of Section~\ref{sec:contraction} therefore carries a third variable beyond
$(N,d)$: B's advantage for expensive perturbations holds only while the
model-sized transfer amortizes, and a slow enough host boundary removes that
amortization even where the contraction is expensive. Third, the frozen
prediction caught the direction on one node but missed the flip, placing the
seed-regenerated $d{=}2048$ cells as B at $D{=}16$ where they measure A and
under-predicting B's boundary penalty by about $90$ to $105$\,ms. The sign that
held for the low-rank arm and for the small model did not hold for the expensive
arm at large $d$.

This locates the flip at or below $9.2$\,GiB/s for the seed-regenerated arm,
and no closer. The boundary measured is one bandwidth point and it is
sockets: the cluster's InfiniBand carried no NCCL traffic on any host drawn,
so whether the flip survives the $12$ to $50$\,GiB/s a production
InfiniBand cluster provides is not established. The frozen model's own flip
estimate for this cell was wrong in the direction that matters, placing it
below the fabric that was measured, so the model cannot place the contour
either. What the measurement establishes is that a boundary exists and which
arms cross it, not where.

\subsection{Where the low-rank rewrite pays}
EGGROLL's motivation is explicitly GPU-shaped \citep{eggroll2025}, so the natural guess was that
a TPU, whose matrix unit makes dense multiplies comparatively cheap, would
narrow the gap or invert it. Figure~\ref{fig:f4} answers: the rewrite pays
\emph{more} on the v5e, not less, both in time (rank-1 at
$0.05$--$0.18\times$ dense against $0.13$--$0.42\times$ on the A100) and
in feasibility (the 16\,GB chip renders the dense baseline infeasible over
most of the grid, drawn as wins because feasibility is the strongest form
of paying off). The platforms move the constants, never the ordering, at
the matched cells of the measured grid. The
comparison is shape-for-shape: no v5e workload was reduced to
accommodate its smaller memory. The same figure prices Qiu's bet
\citep{qiu2025} honestly: seed regeneration costs
$5$--$6\times$ the materializing baseline per device on the TPU against
$1.3$--$3\times$ on the GPU, and its family (mirrored or not) is the
only one that never runs out of memory on either platform, which is
precisely the storage-for-compute trade it advertises. Whether that
$5$--$6\times$ is random-number throughput or the loss of the shared base
multiply the materializing arm keeps is not diagnosed here; the
measurement is end-to-end.

\subsection{Reference implementation comparison}
\input{generated/tb1}
Matched shapes, matched populations, token-position evaluations per
second; nothing here claims
step-equivalence between implementations. The arms differ in antithetic
pairing, shaping and normalization details by design, and the honest unit of
comparison is throughput at the same shapes, which is how both papers report \citep{qiu2025,eggroll2025}.

Beyond parity, the measurements locate two differences from the reference
implementations, stated with their caveats. First, none of the references
shards: at eight devices the instrument reaches
$6\times$ the reference's single-chip ceiling
(Tables~\ref{tab:tb1} and~\ref{tab:tb1b}), which
is a statement about released code, not about what the papers' internal
systems could do. Second, choices the references hardcode are measured to
matter: the contraction placement flips winner by platform
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2}), and EGGROLL's antithetic pairing is built into its
sampler, while the unpaired variant it cannot express has about $1.34\times$
greater update--gradient alignment at matched population in the large-$N$
regime its own paper targets (the fitted alignment curves behind
Figure~\ref{fig:f5}, and $1.36\times$ on the real model,
Table~\ref{tab:tb4}); the pairing halves factor storage in exchange, and at
small $N$ the difference is inside the replicate spread, so this is a choice to expose, not
a defect. Two further implementation-level differences (a TPU compile
failure in the reference's rank-1 path, and its declining embedding
tables) are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:refimpl}; they are
properties of the released implementations, not evidence that the
underlying algorithms require the same restrictions.

Three rows carry the table's weight. Single-device, the instrument runs
within 3\% of EGGROLL's own implementation on the A100
($0.97$--$1.02\times$ across the shapes) and at $0.68$--$0.85\times$ on
the v5e; that is an observed implementation gap between a general API and
a specialized kernel, platform-shaped rather than uniform, and the
experiment does not isolate its cause. At eight
devices it reaches $6\times$ their single-chip ceiling, because no
reference shards and the rows say so rather than hiding it. And evosax, the
incumbent, runs out of memory at $d{=}2048$ on a 16\,GB chip at every
device count: the flattening design decision, measured as a failure row
rather than argued from source code.

\subsection{The shaping barrier, priced}
Section~\ref{sec:design} declared one deliberate synchronization point;
here is its bill, measured in isolation across 84 cells on the TPU. The
communication half is not the story anywhere in the measured range: the
gather runs at
2.4--6\,$\mu$s, flat in $N$ (the payload at $N{=}2^{18}$ is 1\,MB) and
about 3\,$\mu$s dearer at $D{=}8$ than at $D{=}1$. The cost that grows is
the sort, and it is local compute paid redundantly rather than
communication: 12.1\,ms at $N{=}2^{18}$, identical to within $0.3\%$
between one device and eight, because every chip sorts the same
replicated array, about 46\,ns per member on this chip. At populations
from the tens \citet{qiu2025} run to a few thousand, the
whole barrier is under 60\,$\mu$s against generation times in the
milliseconds; at the tens of thousands EGGROLL targets, the sort's
linear growth puts it near a millisecond. It becomes a leading term
only where $N$ reaches the
rank-$r$ regime's extremes and the per-member work is small; at
$N{=}2^{18}$ a 12\,ms sort would rival a low-rank generation itself, and
distributed shaping would become a separate design problem.

An isolation number is the barrier's worst case, since a real generation
may overlap part of the latency behind compute, so we also closed the
caveat in context: identical full generations with shaping on and off,
at the largest cells the sweep measured on this platform, differ by
41\,$\mu$s ($d{=}512$, $N{=}1024$) and 149\,$\mu$s ($d{=}2048$,
$N{=}256$) in the median, both inside their own repeat spreads. At
measured cells the experiment does not resolve the barrier above
generation-to-generation repeat variation (seven repeats per point); the
12\,ms sort stands as the ceiling for a population
regime where a full generation does not fit this hardware anyway.

\subsection{Precision and datatype}
The complete systems-ablation table is in
Appendix~\ref{sec:systems-ablations}.
Two rows earn prose. Compute dtype is a memory choice more than a speed
one in these cells: bfloat16 against float32 is within a few percent on
the TPU, and within 12\% on the A100 (bfloat16 ahead by 8--12\% for the
dense and seed-regenerated arms, parity for low-rank), because at default
matmul precision the tensor core and the matrix unit already downgrade
the multiplies. Matmul
precision, by contrast, is strategy-shaped: forcing full-precision
multiplies is nearly free for the strategies whose time is the shared
base-model multiply, and costs $1.6$--$1.9\times$ for the low-rank
family, whose per-candidate correction multiplies are the operations being
upgraded. A practitioner running defaults gets Figure~\ref{fig:f4}'s
speedups; our scaling sweeps, which pin full precision for cross-device
comparability, were conservative against the low-rank arms specifically.

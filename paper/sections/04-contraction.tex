\section{The contraction question}
\label{sec:contraction}

With the population sharded over $D$ devices (each holds the full weights and
evaluates $N/D$ members; Section~\ref{sec:design}), after evaluation every
device holds fitnesses for its share of the
population, and the update $\sum_i w_i \epsilon_i$ must be assembled (the
contraction). There are two natural placements:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strategy A: gather scalars, replicate the contraction.}
    Each device gathers every member's fitness, then regenerates all $N$
    perturbations and computes the whole contraction itself; nothing is
    combined afterwards, because every device already holds the full sum.
    Communication: scalar gathers only ($8N$ bytes as measured; the model
    below explains the count). This is the
    arrangement behind the folk claim that ES only communicates scalars \citep{salimans2017}.
  \item \textbf{Strategy B: contract partially, then all-reduce.} Each device
    contracts only its own $N/D$ perturbations and the partial sums are added
    across devices (an all-reduce of a model-sized buffer). Communication:
    the full parameter count plus the same scalar gather ($4P{+}4N$ bytes as
    measured); compute per device: $D\times$ less contraction work than A.
\end{itemize}

The trade is compute against communication, so it predicts a crossover in
(population $N$, model size $d$) space rather than one universal winner. The
record committed before measurement contained two claims. The quantitative
one was a byte count, $4N$ for A and $4P$ for B ($P$ parameters, float32).
Counting the payload of every collective in the compiled update corrects it
to $8N$ and $4P{+}4N$: A moves two $4N$-byte gathers, the raw fitnesses for
shaping and the shaped weights for the contraction, and B's fitness gather
rides beside its all-reduce. At $d{=}2048$, $N{=}256$ that is 2{,}048 bytes
measured against 1{,}024 predicted for A, and 100{,}664{,}320 against
100{,}663{,}296 for B, and the same two corrections hold for every strategy
and shape in the grid, exactly. The conclusion is unchanged: 2\,KB against
100\,MB.

The directional claim followed from what each placement buys with those
bytes. B does $1/D$ of A's contraction work, so it should win when
per-candidate contraction is expensive (dense or regenerated perturbations,
larger populations) and the model-sized transfer amortizes. A should return
when that work is already small (the low-rank family) and the transfer is
pure overhead. Both conditions hide a dependence on the interconnect: the
transfer amortizes only while it is cheap against the contraction it saves,
so a slow enough host boundary retracts B's advantage even where the
contraction is expensive (Section~\ref{subsec:boundary}). No quantitative
time model was committed before the runs, and
none is retroactively presented as a prediction. Section~\ref{sec:results}
tests the byte count and this directional claim on both platforms
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2}).

Bytes are what the placement determines and time is what decides a cell, and
the two are not the same statement: a payload reaches a duration through a
rate that depends on the payload's own size and on the fabric, so identical
byte counts can and do give different crossovers on different hardware. The
byte count is therefore the part worth committing in advance, because it is
the part that transfers. What it costs is a separate measurement, one per
fabric, reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} (Table~\ref{tab:tb8}). That
conversion is calibrated on the same platforms it explains, so it is an
account of the crossover rather than a second prediction. It was used as one
once, out of sample, for the host boundary of Section~\ref{subsec:boundary},
where it got the direction right and the magnitude wrong by an order of
magnitude: a payload divided by a benchmarked bandwidth is not what a
collective costs across a socket fabric.

A and B are the endpoints of the placement space, not two arbitrary samples
of it, and measuring the endpoints is the economical choice. Every placement
is a separate implementation against the library's sharding seams and a
separate pass over the $(N,d)$ grid on rented hardware, so the family was
never going to be measured whole; the question is which members buy the most
per pass. These two bound it. A replicates the entire contraction and moves
the fewest bytes any placement can, B splits the contraction as far as $D$
devices allow and pays a model-sized transfer to do it, and every
intermediate trades one for the other between them. A reduce-scatter
followed by an all-gather, with the update applied sharded (the ZeRO
arrangement, \citealp{rajbhandari2020zero}), halves B's bytes at the same
split contraction; reduced-precision or compressed collectives shrink them
further; a hierarchical contraction is A within a host and B across hosts.
Each sits inside the bracket, which is what makes the bracket worth the
machine time.

One thing sits outside it, because it is not a placement at all. Overlapping
B's all-reduce with the next generation's evaluation, the standard
engineering answer to exactly this trade, hides the transfer instead of
shrinking it, so it is bounded by neither endpoint. It is not implemented
here and not measured. If overlap can hide the transfer entirely, the
low-rank side of the crossover belongs to the unoverlapped implementation
rather than to the placement, and that reading stands unrefuted by anything
in this paper.
